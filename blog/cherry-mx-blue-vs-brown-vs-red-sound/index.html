Cherry MX Blue vs Brown vs Red: what each one sounds like

Three real recordings, matched for loudness and played back to back. Pick a strength, press play, and listen for the click, the bump and the glide.

Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer: Cherry MX Blue makes two sounds on every press: a sharp click as the key actuates, about halfway down, then the bottom-out. That makes it the loudest and brightest of the three. MX Brown has a bump you feel but no click, so it sounds like a rounder, muted clack. MX Red is linear, with no bump and no click, so you hear a quick, smooth bottom-out. Blue is the one people notice across a room; Brown and Red sound much closer to each other.

What our three recordings measure

We measured the strikes this page plays. Every pack in Keyslam is leveled to the same loudness, −28 LUFS on a standard typing passage, so none of the three plays louder than the others. What differs is where each strike's energy sits.

Method: the average spectrum of the first 43 ms of each of the 24 regular-key strikes (6 per band) in each pack, 40 Hz to 16 kHz; soft to slam is the median level of each strike's first 40 ms, slam band minus soft band. Measured November 2, 2026 from the files on this page.

Read it this way: a Blue strike puts 71% of its energy between 1 and 4 kHz, close to the range where human hearing is most sensitive, so it sounds sharp and carries. The Brown recording keeps 57% of its energy below 1 kHz, which is why it sounds rounder. The Red sits between them, with 61% in the middle band.

Cherry MX Blue vs Brown sound

Both switches have a bump. The difference is a small part called the click jacket. In a Blue, the jacket rides on the stem, catches on the way down, then snaps free with a click just as the key actuates, and resets with a softer click on the way up. A Brown has the bump without the jacket, so it only makes the two sounds every MX switch makes: the stem bottoming out in the housing, and the stem hitting the top of the housing when the spring pushes it back.

So a Blue adds two clicks to every keystroke and a Brown adds none. Pick Blue if you want every actuation confirmed out loud. Pick Brown if you want feedback you mostly feel, at a volume the people near you can live with.

Cherry MX Blue vs Red sound

This is the widest gap of the three. A Red has no jacket and no bump: a light 45 cN spring and a straight slide to the bottom. All you hear is a quick bottom-out and a quick return. A Blue needs 50 cN to actuate, peaks at 60 cN at the bump and clicks on the way through. Reds suit fast, light typing and gaming. Blues suit people who like to hear each key register.

Red switch vs brown switch sound

The closest pair. Neither clicks, both actuate at 45 cN and both travel 4 mm, so most of what you hear is the bottom-out, and that depends on the keyboard more than the switch. If you bottom out on every key, you'll hear little difference between them. The Brown's bump, 55 cN at its peak, can stop a light typist short of the bottom, which is quieter. In our recordings the Brown is the darker of the two, but they come from different boards and microphones, so part of that gap is the board.

What about MX Black and Gateron Red?

Two more linear switches are in Keyslam: the MX Black pack is a Leopold FC980M PD with Cherry MX Black switches, a linear with a heavier spring than the Red; the Gateron Red pack is a GMMK TKL with Gateron Red switches. Each is a different board and microphone again, so play them next to MX Red to hear how much the board changes a linear sound.

Which Cherry MX switch is quietest?

Of these three, Blue is the loudest because of its click. Red and Brown are close; how hard you bottom out and what the keyboard is made of matter more than the switch. Cherry also makes silent versions, such as MX Silent Red, with dampers built into the stem.

To quiet any board, try rubber O-rings under the keycaps, a desk mat and a lighter touch. If you want the sound without the noise, play a recording through headphones instead. And if all three are too much, the Laptop pack, a close-miked 2018 MacBook Pro, is a duller, subtler tap.

Why soft and slam sound different

Each pack is cut from one real typing recording. Every strike is detected and paired with its key-up, then the strikes are sorted by natural loudness into four bands and each band is leveled to a consistent target. Soft comes from the lighter presses, slam from the hardest ~8%. On a MacBook with Apple's motion sensor, Keyslam picks the band from how hard you actually hit; on other Macs you choose it. Cherry MX sound: questions

Which is louder, Cherry MX Blue or Brown?

Blue. Its click jacket adds a sharp click on the way down and a softer one on the way up, on top of the bottom-out that both switches make. In Keyslam both packs play at the same leveled loudness, so the Blue sounds sharper rather than louder.

Are the Blue, Brown and Red packs recorded on the same keyboard?

No. Each comes from a different CC0 recording on Freesound: MX Blue by EricsSoundschmiede, MX Brown by majod and MX Red by handygaber on a Corsair K95. So the comparison also reflects each board, its keycaps and the microphone.

Do Cherry MX switches make a sound when you let go?

Yes. The spring pushes the stem back up against the top of the housing, and a Blue clicks again as its jacket resets. Keyslam plays real key-up sounds from each recording: 6, 4 and 6 takes in the Blue, Brown and Red packs.
