Real recordings, not synthesized

Nothing here is generated. Each board is cut from one real typing recording that its author released as CC0 (public domain) on Freesound; the typewriters add a second CC0 recording for the bell on Return. Keyslam finds every strike, pairs it with its key-up where the recording has one, and sorts the strikes by natural loudness into four bands: soft (about the 8th to 30th percentile), medium (38th to 62nd), hard (70th to 88th) and slam, the hardest ~8%. Each band is then leveled to a consistent target, and every board to the same overall loudness (-28 LUFS on a standard typing passage), so switching boards doesn't jump in volume. How the simulator works, and its limits

  • Same takes as the Mac app. The browser loads each board as one losslessly compressed file of the same strikes the app plays, then cuts them apart in memory.
  • You choose the strength here. Natural varies each press a little around medium, the way ordinary typing does, with Space and Return landing a bit harder. In the app, MacBooks with Apple's motion sensor pick the strength from how hard you actually hit, read about 800 times a second.
  • Each key keeps its own take and a small fixed pitch offset, so a fast run of letters doesn't sound like one sample on repeat. Keys on the left sit slightly left in stereo, keys on the right slightly right.
  • Browser audio adds delay that depends on your browser and device. The Keyslam keyboard sound app for Mac uses a C audio core with an audio buffer of about 5 ms.
  • Only this tab hears you. Shortcuts with Cmd or Ctrl don't play here, and macOS usually keeps some keys, like Fn and the media keys, to itself.
  • Phones often don't say which key you pressed, so the type box plays a sound for each character instead.
  • Nothing leaves your device. The sounds play in your browser; what you type here isn't sent anywhere.

More on where the recordings come from and how the packs are built is on the Keyslam about page.

Keyboard simulator questions

Is the keyboard simulator free?

Yes. It runs in your browser with no account, no download and no time limit.

Can I use this as a keyboard tester?

Partly. When the browser receives a key that is on the on-screen layout, that key lights up and stays outlined, so it is a quick way to check that the main keys on a keyboard register. The layout has no Esc or function row. It can't measure rollover, ghosting or key chatter, and keys macOS handles itself (Fn, brightness, media keys, Touch ID) usually don't reach a web page. Use a dedicated hardware tester for those.

Can I share a board and strength with someone?

Yes. Pick a board and a strength, then press Copy a link to this sound. The link adds the board and strength to the address, for example ?board=selectric&strength=hard, and opens the simulator with that sound selected.

How close is the browser simulator to the Mac app?

It plays the same recorded strikes and key-up sounds, and each key keeps its own take, as in the app. The differences: here you choose the strength, browser audio adds some delay, and sounds only play in this tab. The Keyslam app plays them for every key you press in any app on your Mac, and on supported MacBooks it reads how hard you type.

Does the on-screen keyboard work on a phone or tablet?

Yes. Tap keys on the on-screen keyboard, or tap the type box and use your phone's own keyboard. Several fingers at once work too.
