Membrane / laptop vs mechanical keyboard sound test

Most people asking this want to know what they'd gain by moving from a flat laptop or membrane keyboard to a mechanical one. The closest thing to a membrane board here is the Laptop pack: a 2018 MacBook Pro butterfly keyboard, close-miked, with short travel, thin caps and no mechanical switch. The mechanical side is a Cherry MX Brown, a tactile switch with no click, which keeps the comparison about the switch and not about a loud click jacket.

The numbers agree with your ears. The laptop strike keeps 90% of its energy below 200 Hz and 2% above 2 kHz; the MX Brown keeps 33% and 6%. At slam the laptop measures -20.8 LUFS and the MX Brown -21.6 LUFS, so what you hear between them is mostly character, not volume.

Listen for three things: the laptop has a short, dull tap with almost no top end; the MX Brown adds a plastic clack as the cap bottoms out; and the MX Brown rings a little longer. To hear the laptop recording key by key, open the MacBook keyboard sound test.

How to run a fair switch sound test

  1. Use headphones and set the volume once. Changing it between boards makes the louder one sound "better".
  2. Keep the strength the same. The A/B player locks it. The Play buttons in the tables vary each press a little, like normal typing, so use the A/B player for a strict comparison.
  3. Listen for one thing at a time: the click before bottom-out (only clicky switches have it), the pitch of the bottom-out, the ring after the key, and the sound on the way back up.
  4. Go blind. Pick two close boards, like two tactile ones, and play X ten times. If you score around 5 of 10, you probably can't hear a difference, and that's useful to know before you buy a keyboard.

How we measured

On November 2, 2026 we rendered the same 40-key passage from each board's soft, medium, hard and slam strikes: the letters of “the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog”, repeated to 40 keys, one every 150 ms (about 80 words per minute) with its key-up 80 ms later, at 100% volume, the way this page plays them, minus its small random variations. Each passage was read with ffmpeg's EBU R128 meter for integrated loudness and true peak. The frequency numbers come from the first 85 ms of every medium and hard strike, median across strikes. All 15 boards were measured the same way on the same day.

The packs in the Keyslam keyboard sound app for Mac are cut from one real typing recording each (the typewriters add a bell recording for Return). Keyslam sorts the strikes by natural loudness into soft, medium, hard and slam (the hardest ~8%) and levels each band, and every pack's standard typing passage is leveled to the same loudness (-28 LUFS). That is why the medium column is so even. More on the recordings is on the Keyslam about page.

Keyboard sound test questions

Which keyboard sound is the quietest?

At medium strength the 15 boards measure within 2.2 LU of each other (-28.7 to -26.5 LUFS), because every pack is leveled to the same loudness. The differences show when you hit hard: Topre and Unicomp stay the quietest at slam (-23.1 and -22.9 LUFS), and Vintage Technics and Olivetti get the loudest (-20.5 and -20.0 LUFS).

Why are the boards matched at medium but not at slam?

Because the leveling targets medium. Keyslam sets every pack so a standard medium-strength typing passage measures -28 LUFS, which is why the medium column sits within 2.2 LU. Within each band, every take is moved most of the way, not all the way, toward the band's level target, so part of each recording's natural spread survives, and that spread shows in the slam column. At medium, this is a test of character: click, thock, ring and the key-up sound.

What do LUFS, LU and dBTP mean?

LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale) measures perceived loudness, with numbers closer to zero being louder. A difference of 1 LU equals 1 dB. dBTP is the true peak, the highest point of the waveform, where 0 is the top before clipping.

Is a laptop keyboard sound test useful if I already own a MacBook?

Yes. The Laptop pack is a recording of a real 2018 MacBook Pro butterfly keyboard, so it shows what a gentle "just confirm the press" sound is like next to a mechanical board, at the same loudness.
